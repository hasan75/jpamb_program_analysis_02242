\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Testing static analyzers against a reference}
\citet{klinger2019} insert synthesized assertions into correct C
programs and run six verifiers and analyzers on the variants; an analyzer that
disagrees with the majority points to a soundness or precision issue. \citet{cuoq2012} test Frama-C on random Csmith programs, using the value
analysis as an interpreter and comparing with compiled execution, and \citet{taneja2020} compare LLVM's dataflow analyses, such as known bits and
integer ranges, with maximally precise results computed by an SMT solver. All
three need a reference (other analyzers, an execution, or a solver), whereas
our oracle is the analyzer itself on an equivalent method.

\paragraph{Metamorphic testing of analyzers}
Metamorphic testing replaces an oracle by relations between the outputs for
related inputs~\citep{chen2018}. Interrogation testing~\citep{kaindlstorfer2024}
applies it to eight C/C++ analyzers, including the abstract interpreters Clam
and MOPSA: it adds,
strengthens or weakens assertions and assumptions, partly based on which
assertions the analyzer has already proven, and checks set inclusions between
the failing assertions. Its transformations change the specification with a
predicted effect, while ours preserve the method's behavior and compare
predicted outcome sets. Statfier~\citep{zhang2023statfier} applies
semantics-preserving transformations to programs from the test suites of PMD,
SpotBugs, Infer, SonarQube and CheckStyle, at warning lines and their backward
slices, and reports a bug when the number or type of warnings changes. Both
use the analyzer's own results to decide what to transform. We choose sites
from a fixed analysis's abstract state, which also applies where the analyzer
reports nothing, compare this choice with random and syntax-based selection
under one budget, and find that on JPAMB it does worse than random
(\cref{sec:rq1}).

\paragraph{Equivalence-based testing of compilers and models}
Equivalence modulo inputs~\citep{le2014emi} prunes code that is not executed on
given inputs and compares the outputs of the compiled programs; like our
guide, it uses information about the program (coverage) to choose where to
mutate, and its authors suggest adapting the idea to analysis systems. GLFuzz~\citep{donaldson2017} applies
semantics-preserving transformations to graphics shaders and compares the
rendered images. \citet{rabin2021} show that neural models of code
often change their method-name predictions under such transformations. Our
rules are of the same kind, restricted to integer conditions so that their
equivalence can be argued and tested.

\paragraph{Earlier DTU work}
\citet{chand2024} compare an LLM and Infer on null pointer exceptions in
LLM-generated Java, with a fuzzer as ground truth, and check robustness by
renaming; \citet{domingues2025} use abstract states to choose fuzzer inputs on
JPAMB. As argued in \cref{sec:intro}, neither chooses where to rewrite.
